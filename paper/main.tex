\title{Elephant Herd:\\
Partitioning a Cached Corpus Across Listening Agents}
\author{Florin Birgu}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Keeping an entire corpus resident in a language model's context, rather than
retrieving fragments of it per query, was until recently priced out of existence. Two
independent cost reductions changed that: capable small models, roughly 67$\times$
cheaper per token than frontier models, and prompt caching, a further 5$\times$ on
repeated reads. Together a 334$\times$ reduction, which takes a turn over a
1.19M-token corpus from \$5.95 to \$0.018.

We describe \textbf{Elephant Herd}, which partitions a corpus across $N$ persistent
listeners, each holding its partition as a cached prefix, and broadcasts every
conversational turn to all of them. Each listener contributes a cited fact or stays
silent, and a leader merges what returns into a briefing.

Measured on RULER with seven listener models, three results. \textbf{Partitioning
does not improve retrieval}: a 7-way herd and a single model reading everything both
score 100\% at 131K, 256K and 512K tokens, at the same cost; the case for partitioning
is capacity and specialisation, not recall. \textbf{Partitioning does not break chained
facts either}, provided each listener is asked for relevant material rather than for
the answer: on a variable-tracking chain split across seven partitions, one round
scores 9 or 10 out of 10 for five of seven models. \textbf{Cheap listeners match a
frontier one}: four models costing \$0.004 to \$0.086 per query score the same as
Claude Sonnet 5 at \$0.357. We also report how an earlier version of our own harness
produced 0\% on the chain task for all seven models, Sonnet included, and why a uniform
zero across vendors should be read as a fault in the harness before a property of the
method.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

A project folder grows for months. The assistant working in it sees whatever a
retriever fetched for the current question, and nothing else. A decision written down
in March is never read again.

This looks like a retrieval problem and is usually treated as one. We suggest it is a
different problem. Retrieval runs when somebody asks a question, and most of the time
nobody asks. A note about pricing does not surface during a conversation about pricing
unless someone stops and goes looking for it.

Elephant Herd inverts this. Agents hold the notes in context and observe the
conversation. When one recognises something relevant, it says so. Nobody has to ask.

What makes this possible now is not a new algorithm. Context stopped being expensive.

\centering

Configuration & Cost per turn, 1.19M-token corpus \\
\midrule
Frontier model, uncached & \$5.95 \\
Frontier model, cached & \$0.60 \\

\textbf{Small model, cached} & \textbf{\$0.018} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Two effects multiply: model choice ($\sim$67$\times$) and caching
($\sim$5$\times$). The first made a resident corpus viable. The second made it cheap to

\end{table}

The cost argument only holds if a cheap listener is adequate for the task. We measure
that directly in Section~\ref{sec:models}, rather than assuming it from price or from
published long-context scores.

\section{System}

A \emph{herd} is $N$ \emph{elephants} plus a \emph{leader}.

Each elephant holds one partition as a cached prefix. Partitions follow domain
boundaries wherever possible, splitting only when a domain exceeds the context window;
assembly is byte-deterministic so the cached prefix stays valid across turns.

Every turn is broadcast to all elephants. A local turn gate drops conversational filler
before it reaches them, which costs nothing and removes a class of false contribution:
an elephant cannot speak on a turn it never received.

Each elephant replies with a cited contribution or the literal token
\texttt{<silent/>}. Silence is the expected case.

The leader merges contributions into a briefing for the calling agent. The audience is
a model rather than a person, so the output is facts with source paths and no prose.
When nothing was contributed it emits nothing. When two elephants disagree it reports
both rather than picking one.

An optional iterated mode (\texttt{-{}-deep}) re-broadcasts the findings so far, so an
elephant can extend what another found. None of the results below needed it.

\subsection{Implementation notes that turned out to matter}

\textbf{Provider pinning is experimental control, not cost hygiene.} The same model is
served by roughly 30 hosts at cache-read prices spanning 10$\times$ and quantizations
from fp4 to bf16. Unpinned routing varies recall between calls.

\textbf{Listed cache prices are unreliable.} One provider billed 4.2$\times$ its
advertised cache-read rate, because a five-minute TTL means calls inside the window
re-create the cache while still reporting a 100\% cache hit.

\textbf{Truncation is indistinguishable from silence.} Reasoning tokens come out of the
same budget as the reply. A listener that exhausts that budget while reasoning returns
an empty string, which looks exactly like a deliberate \texttt{<silent/>}. Unless the
finish reason is checked, a budget problem is scored as a recall failure. Section
\ref{sec:harness} shows this happening at a 2{,}000-token cap.

\textbf{Ask listeners for material, not answers.} A listener told to answer or stay
silent will correctly stay silent when its partition holds only part of an answer, and
the part never reaches the merge. Section~\ref{sec:harness} measures the effect.

\textbf{Reasoning must be enabled.} Disabling it saves around 200 output tokens per
silent turn, worth \$0.0001 against a \$0.0106 cache read, and loses correct answers.

\section{Experiments}

RULER \cite{ruler} (Apache 2.0). \texttt{single} is one call over the full context;
\texttt{herd} is 7 calls over a seventh each, reading the same total tokens, with the
replies concatenated and scored by RULER's rule that every gold string must appear.
Every listener is pinned to one host, so routing cannot vary quantization or recall
between calls.

Sections \ref{sec:retrieval} and \ref{sec:length} use GLM-5.3-Flash on haystacks
generated from Paul Graham essays. Sections \ref{sec:models} and \ref{sec:harness} use
seven models on the public 128K RULER set published as
\texttt{self-long/RULER-llama3-1M}, $n=10$ per cell. Costs are OpenRouter prices in
September 2026 for one uncached query of about 128K input tokens; token counts are
given so they can be recomputed as prices change.

\subsection{Retrieval is preserved}\label{sec:retrieval}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Task & Single & Herd & Mean speakers \\
\midrule
Single needle & 100\% & 100\% & 1.0 / 7 \\
Needle among distractors & 100\% & 100\% & 2.9 / 7 \\
Four scattered values & 100\% & 100\% & 3.8 / 7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{$n=10$ per task at 131K tokens.}
\end{table}

The multi-value case is the notable one: the four gold values are scattered, scoring
requires all of them, and roughly 3.8 elephants spoke per sample, so the values
genuinely landed in different partitions and the merge recovered them. Silence
discriminated cleanly, with zero false contributions across 70 listener calls on the
single-needle task.

\subsection{Context length: no separation up to 512K}\label{sec:length}

If partitioning helped recall, the advantage should appear as the single context grows
while each partition stays small. It does not.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Context & Single & Herd & Cost, single & Cost, herd \\
\midrule
131K & 100\% & 100\% & \$0.0072 & \$0.0055 \\
256K & 100\% & 100\% & \$0.0121 & \$0.0126 \\
512K & 100\% & 100\% & \$0.0219 & \$0.0235 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{$n=8$ per length. No degradation, so the curves never diverge.}
\end{table}

\textbf{Partitioning does not improve retrieval at any length we can test.} The reasons
to partition are capacity and specialisation: a corpus larger than any context window
has no single-context arm at all, and partitions mapping to real domains make ``which
specialist should have known this?'' answerable, which is what silence and attribution
depend on. Neither is expressible in RULER, whose haystacks are arbitrary essay chunks.

\subsection{Chained facts survive partitioning}\label{sec:models}

Variable tracking asks for every variable that ends up holding a value, where the value
is assigned once and then passed along a chain: \texttt{VAR A = 46208},
\texttt{VAR B = VAR A}, and so on, five variables in all. The links are scattered
through 128K tokens of filler, so with seven partitions no listener sees the whole
chain. This has the same shape as tracing a symbol through a codebase or following a
decision through notes that refer to each other: the answer is written down nowhere,
and has to be assembled.

Each listener received its partition, the question, and this instruction:

\begin{quote}\small
You hold part of a longer text. Report anything in your part that is relevant to the
question, quoted exactly. Include things that only point at something else instead of
answering it. Others hold the rest of the text, so do not try to work out the final
answer. Nothing relevant? Reply: \texttt{<silent/>}
\end{quote}

\noindent with a reply budget of 20{,}000 tokens, one round, no iteration.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Listener & One fact & Several facts & Chain & Cost per query \\
\midrule
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash   & 10/10 & 10/10 & 10/10 & \$0.011 \\
Gemini 3.7 Flash      & 10/10 & 10/10 & 10/10 & \$0.086 \\
Claude Sonnet 5       & 10/10 & 10/10 &  9/10 & \$0.357 \\
GLM-5.3-Flash         & 10/10 & 10/10 &  9/10 & \$0.016 \\
Qwen3.7-Flash         & 10/10 & 10/10 &  9/10 & \$0.004 \\
Xiaomi MiMo-v2.5      & 10/10 &  8/10 &  2/10 & \$0.012 \\
Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite & 10/10 &  9/10 &  0/10 & \$0.016 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Herd of 7, 128K tokens, one round. ``One fact'' is RULER
\texttt{niah\_single\_2}, ``several facts'' \texttt{niah\_multivalue}, ``chain''
\texttt{vt}. Cost is for the chain task.}
\end{table}

Five of seven listeners recover the chain in a single round, so partitioning does not
break linked questions. A single call over the whole context scored 3 of 3 (GLM-5.3-Flash) and 2 of 2
(DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash) on the same data, so the herd matches the unpartitioned case;
that comparison is small and was run under the old instruction below, which suits a
single reader since it can see the whole chain.

The listeners that succeed include four whose cost per query is between a fourth and a
ninetieth of Sonnet 5's, with the same or one more correct answer. Price did not predict
the two failures, and neither did the other two tasks: Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite is near
perfect on one fact and several facts and scores zero on the chain. Both failing models
also fail under the old instruction below, so the change of instruction does not
explain them; we have not established what does.

\subsection{A harness fault that looked like a finding}\label{sec:harness}

An earlier version of this paper reported that partitioning destroys chained facts:
0\% for the herd against 100\% for a single model, recovered only by iterating the
herd at about three times the cost. That result came from two choices in our harness,
not from partitioning.

The first was the instruction. Listeners were told to answer the question from their
part, or reply \texttt{<silent/>}. A listener holding \texttt{VAR LKDVP = VAR TWGEN}
has no answer to ``which variables equal 46208?'', since its line holds no value, so
silence was the correct reply and every listener gave it. The second was a reply
budget of 2{,}000 tokens, which reasoning could exhaust before any answer was written,
returning an empty string scored as silence.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Listener & Old: answer or silent, 2K & New: report relevant, 20K \\
\midrule
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash   & 0/10 & 10/10 \\
Gemini 3.7 Flash      & 0/10 & 10/10 \\
Claude Sonnet 5       & 0/10 &  9/10 \\
GLM-5.3-Flash         & 1/10 &  9/10 \\
Qwen3.7-Flash         & 0/10 &  9/10 \\
Xiaomi MiMo-v2.5      & 0/9  &  2/10 \\
Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite & 0/10 &  0/10 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Chain task, same data and settings apart from the instruction and the reply
budget. One Xiaomi sample was lost to a provider error. Under the old settings the one
fact and several facts tasks scored 9 or 10 out of 10 for every model, so the fault
was specific to answers spread across partitions.}
\end{table}

In a smaller run ($n=3$), changing the instruction alone lifted Sonnet 5 and Gemini
3.7 Flash to 3 of 3 but the three cheapest models only to 1 of 3; adding the larger
budget lifted the two cheap models we retested to 3 of 3. Both changes were needed.

Two lessons generalise. A listener that follows a strict silence instruction faithfully
hides partial information the system needs, while one that ignores it is rewarded; a
benchmark built that way measures obedience. And seven models from six vendors scoring
zero on the same task is more likely a fault in the harness than a property of the
method. We read it as the latter for several weeks.

Results from the earlier version that depended on this harness are withdrawn: the
0\% single-round chain result, the scaling of iteration rounds with chain length, and a
finding about the rule for halting iteration. Iteration was compensating for the
harness, and is not needed for any result reported here.

\section{Limitations}

$n$ is 10 per cell; single-sample differences between listeners are not meaningful.
The chain comparison uses one length (128K), one partition count (7) and one chain
shape. Retrieval and context-length results use one model and one haystack type.

The RULER instruction above is written for this benchmark. The deployed system asks
listeners for a cited contribution of one to three sentences, and has not been
evaluated with the same rigour.

We do not know why two listeners fail on chains under both instructions. Our count of
how many listeners spoke treats an empty reply as speaking, so the speaker counts in
Table~2 are upper bounds, and the count cannot be used to explain those failures.

RULER cannot test the system's central design choice. Its haystacks have no domain
structure, so semantic partitioning is not expressible and the results measure
\emph{arbitrary} partitioning, a lower bound. Its needles also share vocabulary with the
question, which is the easy case; NoLiMa \cite{nolima} shows accuracy falling sharply
once that overlap is removed. Whether domain-coherent partitions do better is untested
and requires a corpus with real domains.

There is no retrieval baseline. The claim here concerns partitioning versus whole
context, not versus retrieval.

The conversational setting that motivates the system is not benchmarked; RULER is
single-query. Whether a listener recognises relevance when \emph{no question was asked}
is the claim the system rests on and the one least tested here.

\section{Related work}

Closest is ExtAgents \cite{extagents}, which partitions external knowledge across agents
and broadcasts a query, but instantiates agents per query rather than persisting them.
LLM-based multi-agent blackboard systems \cite{blackboard} have partition-owning agents
that volunteer to a central agent, but retrieve on demand rather than holding knowledge
resident.
Cache-Augmented Generation \cite{cag} argues for a resident corpus in a single context,
and LOFT \cite{loft} benchmarks whether long-context models can subsume retrieval
outright.
Overhearing agents \cite{overhearing} survey single assistants that monitor ambient
activity and intervene when useful, and identify ``when to speak'' as open.

The contribution here is the persistence-via-cache economics, the measurement that
cheap listeners suffice, and the finding that the listener's instruction, not the
partitioning, decides whether facts spread across partitions survive.
